# Instructor Feedback on Milestone 1

## Collective Feedback on Topic, Scholarly Context, and Collaboration Plan

I like your shared focus on discrimination and ethics in entertainment, especially the opportunity to connect sports, games, and film through questions about how inequality becomes visible. Your projects do not need to examine the same medium, but you might clarify the shared question a little more. "Social issues in entertainment" is still broad, so you could explain which forms of representation or institutional practice you will examine and what falls outside your scope.

I think your attention to subtle and direct discrimination, intersecting identities, and the ethics of representing real people gives you a useful starting point for cultural complexity. The next step is to show how those concerns will shape the data itself. Who defines a category, what evidence supports an interpretation, and where might another researcher disagree? The strongest version of this project will not simply label examples as discriminatory, but make the interpretive work behind those labels visible.

I appreciated that you explained how scholarship relates to the individual projects. Since you are working with identity and discrimination, the scholarship should help shape your categories and ethical decisions from the beginning, rather than only contextualize the results afterward.

The main piece to clarify is how each custom dataset and audit will become concrete. Most of the individual proposals identify important topics but do not yet name accessible sources or define a feasible object of study. Before committing to a plan, each person might begin by locating and inspecting the proposed existing dataset to see whether it contains usable records and documentation. It would also be useful to consider where computation could assist the work beyond spreadsheet entry.

Your group chat and individual directories sound like useful starting points. I would prefer you to do the work in this shared repository rather than create a second repository for preliminary materials, since that would divide your project history and make contributions harder to follow. You can use branches and pull requests for work that needs review. I would generally recommend using group logs and commits to document everyone's contributions, including work outside GitHub.

Overall, I think you have a promising collective direction and looking forward to seeing how this develops!

You could allow multiple interpretations or uncertainty rather than force every example into one label. Steam may provide useful platform metadata if you choose games available there, but platform metadata alone will not tell you how a game represents or critiques discrimination.

Computation could help organize your observations, search accessible text, or structure annotations through a CLI or HTML form. It would be useful to distinguish what the tools might assist with from what would still require playing, viewing, or close interpretation. Jankowski seems like a useful foundation for thinking about rhetoric, plot, social context, and authorship as interpretive categories rather than assuming that putting them into a dataset makes them purely quantitative.

The final piece to consider is the existing data you might audit. We were unable to access a dataset through the source currently cited, and Jankowski's article appears to be scholarship framing your work rather than necessarily a reusable dataset. Overall, though I think you have a promising direction if you clarify the materials, categories, and evidence you will use to support your interpretations. It would be helpful to confirm that a suitable dataset exists before you commit to this plan. The Collins scholarship you identified gives you a useful starting point for thinking about gender representation and content analysis. If you retain other dimensions after narrowing the project, additional work specific to those questions could help you develop the categories, but you do not need to solve every possible form of representation at this stage. I think narrowing will give you more room for cultural complexity than trying to classify every possible issue at once. Overall, though I think you have a promising direction and happy to discuss further if you would like help identifying more computational methods or datasets!

Depending on the available evidence, that might include contract status, public accounts of negotiations, or institutional context. Before deciding on the sample, it would be useful to see which performance and contract fields are publicly available and comparable. You might keep source descriptions separate from your interpretations, and I would avoid inferring racial identity from names, photographs, or automated tools. You could also preserve the terminology and evidence behind any demographic classification, including unknown or contested cases.

I would also be careful about promising a clear-cut conclusion about discrimination. A salary difference alone does not explain why it exists. You might consider what the available evidence supports and what remains outside the project. The cultural question could focus on how the dataset makes inequality measurable, which contextual relationships it preserves, and which it leaves out.

Computation could help you inspect missing values, match player records, or compare how fields are defined across sources. You could also use code to assist with recording and checking your contextual annotations. For the audit, you might investigate how the Dataverse data was assembled, how its categories were defined, and how selected records could be verified. The Masella research and accompanying Harvard Dataverse source give you a particularly useful starting point because you can consider both the scholarly argument and the data used to support it.
